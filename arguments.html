arguments

Ten arguments about God. Five for each side. Each one comes with the best answer to it.

## before you argue

No argument here is a proof. Each one is strong, but the other side has a good answer. To win a talk, know that answer first. And agree with what is true.

One fact: in a 2020 survey of 1,770 philosophers, about two out of three said they accept or lean towards atheism. Many people agreeing is not an argument. But you should know it. source

## for the believer, against the atheist

1. The universe is set up just right

For life to exist, a few numbers in physics must be almost exactly what they are. Change them a little and there are no stars or no atoms. A designer explains this better than luck.

Reply: we do not know what the other possible numbers could have been, so we cannot say the odds are low. Also, there may be many universes. We can only be in one where life works.

SEP: Fine-Tuning

2. Everything needs a reason

Each thing exists because of something else, or because it has to exist. The universe does not have to exist. So something that must exist is behind it (Leibniz).

Reply: maybe the universe just exists, with no reason (Russell). Also, what is true of each part is not always true of the whole. And a first cause is still not a God who cares about you.

SEP: Cosmological Argument · Russell–Copleston, 1948

3. Right and wrong are real

Cruelty feels wrong in every culture and every age. If that is a real fact, it needs a base. A good God is a better base than evolution, which only cares about survival.

Reply: is something good because God says so, or does God say so because it is good? Either answer hurts. Also, some thinkers, like Parfit, say right and wrong are real without any God.

SEP: Moral Arguments · SEP: Theological Voluntarism

4. The mind

Science explains what the brain does. It does not explain why that feels like something from the inside (Nagel, Chalmers). Mind coming first fits that better.

Reply: not knowing yet is not proof of God. Science keeps making progress. And even if the mind is basic, that does not lead to a God.

SEP: Consciousness

5. Can we trust our own thinking?

If our brains were built only to survive, why trust them on big questions about reality? That includes the claim that nature is all there is (Plantinga).

Reply: true beliefs usually help you survive, so evolution should favour good thinking. Fitelson and Sober said Plantinga never proved his numbers.

Evolutionary argument against naturalism

## for the atheist, against the believer

1. Suffering

Some pain seems to serve no purpose, like an animal burning alone in a forest fire (Rowe). A God who is all-powerful and all-good would stop it.

Reply: a world with real choice and growth may need real risk (Hick). Also, we are small and may not see God's reasons. The comeback: if we cannot see his reasons, we cannot be sure about right and wrong either.

SEP: Problem of Evil · SEP: Skeptical Theism

2. A hidden God

A loving God would let every open-minded person know him. But honest people search and find nothing. So there is no such God (Schellenberg).

Reply: knowing that God exists is not the same as trusting him. Proof so strong that nobody can doubt might force obedience, not love. And God may have reasons we cannot see.

SEP: Hiddenness of God

3. The gaps keep shrinking

People once blamed gods for lightning, plague and the variety of life. Each one got a natural cause. Why should the universe itself be different?

Reply: that is a pattern, not a proof. Science needs laws and a universe to start with. Why those exist is still open. Both sides have something they cannot explain.

SEP: Religion and Science

4. Faith follows birthplace

Religions disagree with each other. Most people keep the faith of the place they were born. That looks like upbringing, not discovery.

Reply: where a belief came from does not show it is false. The same is true for an atheist raised in a home without faith.

SEP: Religious Diversity

5. Miracles

A miracle story must beat everything we have ever seen. False stories are common. Broken laws of nature are not (Hume).

Reply: saying we have never seen an exception already assumes there are none. Critics such as Earman say the argument fails. Each report has to be judged on its own evidence.

SEP: Miracles

## quick replies

Short lines that fit almost any talk, with a believer or an atheist. Say them calmly. They work because they are fair.

1. What would change your mind?

Use when: they will not move. It shows if their view is open or closed.

2. What do you mean by that word?

Use when: “God”, “faith” or “proof” means something different to each of you.

3. How you got a belief is not the same as whether it is true.

Use when: they attack where your belief came from, like your family or country.

4. We both have something we cannot explain.

Use when: they call your side the one with gaps.

5. Whoever makes a claim has to back it. What is yours?

Use when: they say the other side must prove everything.

6. “We don’t know yet” is an honest answer.

Use when: someone fills a gap with certainty, from either side.

7. Many people believing it does not make it true. Few people believing it does not make it false.

Use when: they quote numbers or popularity.

8. Good and bad people are on both sides. Let’s judge the argument.

Use when: they point at bad things done by believers or atheists.

9. Say it in its strongest form. I will answer that one.

Use when: you feel you are beating a weak version of your point.

10. I agree with that part. Here is where we split.

Use when: it gets heated. Agreeing first makes people listen.

## sanskrit, for a fake godman

Five short verses to remember. Read the bright line out loud, one small part at a time. The Sanskrit script is underneath. None of them names fake monks. They are general lines, and the “use when” part is how to apply them. Stay calm. Question his behaviour, not anyone’s faith.

1. Test the teacher

tad vid-dhi pra-ni-pa-te-na pa-ri-prash-ne-na se-va-ya

तद्विद्धि प्रणिपातेन परिप्रश्नेन सेवया

Means: Learn the truth by going to a teacher with respect, with questions, and with service. Those who have really seen the truth can teach you.

Use when: A real teacher welcomes questions. If he gets angry at plain questions, that is a warning.

Bhagavad Gita 4.34

2. Look for show-off and pride

dam-bho dar-po bhi-ma-nash cha kro-dhah pa-rush-yam e-va cha

दम्भो दर्पोऽभिमानश्च क्रोधः पारुष्यमेव च

Means: Pretending, pride, conceit, anger and harsh speech. The Gita lists these as marks of the wrong path.

Use when: He talks of peace but shows pride, anger or harsh words. Judge his conduct, not his robes or his speeches.

Bhagavad Gita 16.4

3. Hold him to the real list

a-ma-nit-vam a-dam-bhit-vam a-him-sa kshan-tir ar-ja-vam

अमानित्वमदम्भित्वमहिंसा क्षान्तिरार्जवम्

Means: No pride. No pretending. No harm to others. Patience. Straight, simple honesty.

Use when: Ask: does he live like this? The Gita itself says knowledge shows up as this kind of character.

Bhagavad Gita 13.8

4. A leader sets the example

yad yad a-cha-ra-ti shresh-thas tat tad e-ve-ta-ro ja-nah

यद्यदाचरति श्रेष्ठस्तत्तदेवेतरो जनः

Means: Whatever a great person does, other people copy.

Use when: He tells followers to give up money or comfort but lives in luxury. Those he leads will copy him, so his own conduct is the test.

Bhagavad Gita 3.21

5. Truth wins

sat-yam e-va ja-ya-te na-nri-tam

सत्यमेव जयते नानृतम्

Means: Truth alone wins, not untruth.

Use when: He dodges plain questions about money, miracles or facts. Ask for the truth in the open.

Mundaka Upanishad 3.1.6
